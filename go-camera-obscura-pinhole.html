This simulation turns a whole room into a pinhole camera, a camera obscura. The room is dark, and light from the street enters only through a small hole in the front wall. An upside-down picture of the street -- the sky, a tree, a person and a car -- appears on the back wall.

Every point of the street sends light in all directions. Almost all of it is stopped by the front wall; only the narrow bundle of rays that passes through the hole reaches the back wall. Because light travels in straight lines, the rays from a high point arrive low on the back wall and the rays from a low point arrive high, so the image is upside down. Left and right are not swapped: an object on your right when you look out through the hole also appears on your right when you face the back wall.

The side view follows three colored points of one object. Each point can send a single ray through the center of the hole, or a spread of rays, most of which are stopped by the wall. The street and the room are drawn at different scales so that both fit; the rays still go straight through the hole because the triangles on both sides are similar.

A point at a distance `d_o` in front of the hole is imaged at a distance `d_i` behind it, with the magnification

`m = -d_i/d_o`,

so an object of height `h_o` has an image of height `h_i = |m| h_o`. The minus sign means the image is inverted.

A hole of diameter `a` lets through a cone of rays from each point, which lands as a small spot rather than a point. Its diameter is

`b = a (1 + d_i/d_o)`,

so a larger hole gives a blurrier image. A very small hole runs into diffraction: light spreads out after passing through it, by an angle of about `1.22 lambda/a`, which adds a blur spot of diameter about

`b_d ~~ 2.44 lambda d_i/a`.

The two effects pull in opposite directions, so there is a sharpest hole size,

`a_"best" = sqrt(2.44 lambda d_i/(1 + d_i/d_o))`,

which is only about 2 mm for a room 4 m deep. The brightness of the image is proportional to the area of the hole and falls off with the square of the distance to the back wall,

`E prop a^2/d_i^2`,

so the sharpest image is also a dim one. That is why the room must be really dark, and why the image seems to appear slowly as the eyes adapt to the dark.

Users can change the size of the hole, the distance to the street and the depth of the room, choose single rays or a spread of rays from each point, follow the person, the tree or the taxi, let light leak into the room, and change the sensitivity of the eyes to see how a dim image becomes visible after dark adaptation.

This simulation is useful for studying the straight-line propagation of light, pinhole cameras and the camera obscura, real and inverted images, magnification, the trade-off between sharpness and brightness, and diffraction.

  1. Choose a single ray from each point. Explain, using the rays, why the image on the back wall is upside down.
  2. Use the readout to find the magnification. How tall is the image of the 1.78 m person? Check with `h_i = |m| h_o`.
  3. Press Large hole and choose a spread of rays. Why is the image blurry now? Compare the blur in the readout with `b = a (1 + d_i/d_o)`.
  4. Press Small hole. The image is sharper but darker. By what factor did the brightness change? Raise the eyes' sensitivity to see the image again.
  5. Turn diffraction on and make the hole smaller and smaller. What happens to the sharpness? Explain why there is a sharpest hole size.
  6. Let light leak into the room. What happens to the image, and why must the room be dark?
