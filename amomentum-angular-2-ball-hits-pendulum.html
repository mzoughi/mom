A Ball Hits a Pendulum: Angular Momentum About the Pivot

side view: a ball slides along a track and hits a pendulum (a heavy rod with a ball at its end)

This simulation shows a ball sliding along a horizontal track and hitting a pendulum: a heavy rod of mass `M_r` and length `L`, with a ball of mass `M_b` at its end, hanging from a pivot. The pendulum's moment of inertia about the pivot is

`I = 1/3 M_r L^2 + M_b (L^2 + 2/5 R_b^2)`.

Which quantity is conserved? During the collision the pivot holds the top of the pendulum in place, so it can push on the pendulum with a large force. That force comes from outside the system, so the total linear momentum is not conserved. But it acts at the pivot itself, so it has no torque about the pivot: the angular momentum about the pivot is conserved. For a ball of mass `m` and speed `v` hitting at a depth `d` below the pivot,

`m v d = m v' d + I omega'`.

With a coefficient of restitution `e` (the speed at which they separate, over the speed at which they meet), `omega' d - v' = e v`, which gives

`omega' = (m v d (1 + e))/(I + m d^2)`,   `v' = v (m d^2 - e I)/(I + m d^2)`.

If the ball sticks to the pendulum (a ballistic pendulum), `omega' = m v d / (I + m d^2)`.

The pivot's push and the sweet spot. The impulse from the pivot is whatever is needed to make up the change in total momentum, `J_"pivot" = (m v' + M omega' d_(cm)) - m v`, where `M` is the pendulum's mass and `d_(cm)` the depth of its centre of mass. It vanishes when the ball hits the centre of percussion,

`d_(cp) = I/(M d_(cm))`,

which is the "sweet spot" of a bat or a racket: hit there, and the hands (the pivot) feel no jolt. Hit above the sweet spot, closer to the pivot, and the pivot must push backward to hold the top in place; hit below it, and the pivot pushes forward. The push is `J_"pivot" = omega' M d_(cm) (1 - d_(cp)/d)`.

The swing. After the collision, the pendulum (with the ball, if it stuck) swings under gravity, `I theta'' = -M g d_(cm) sin theta`. Energy is conserved during the swing, so the highest angle follows from `1/2 I omega'^2 = M g d_(cm) (1 - cos theta_"max")`.
