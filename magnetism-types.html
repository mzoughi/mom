Types of Magnetism - Simulation:

  1. Choose a diamagnetic sample (bismuth) and raise the applied field. Which way do the induced atomic moments point compared with B? Is the sample pulled into or pushed out of the field?
  2. For bismuth, double the field from 0.5 T to 1.0 T. By what factor do the magnetization and the force change? Explain using F = χAB²/2μ₀.
  3. Change the temperature of the bismuth sample from 4 K to 1100 K. What happens to its susceptibility? What does this tell you about diamagnetism?
  4. Choose the YBCO superconductor and cool it below 92 K with a field applied. What happens to the field lines and to the balance reading? Why is a superconductor called a perfect diamagnet?
  5. Choose copper sulfate at 293 K. Raise the field. How do the atomic moments respond, and is the sample attracted or repelled?
  6. Keep B = 1 T and cool the copper sulfate from 293 K to 77 K. By what factor does χ change? Does this agree with Curie's law?
  7. Choose gadolinium sulfate at 4 K and sweep the field. Why is the M–B graph no longer a straight line?
  8. For a paramagnet, what happens to the magnetization when the field is switched off? Why?
  9. Choose soft iron. Starting from B = 0, raise the field slowly and watch the domains. Describe how the domains change. Compare the size of the magnetization with that of the paramagnetic samples.
  10. Press Sweep with hardened steel. Sketch the M–B loop and mark the remanent magnetization and the coercive field.
  11. Compare the hysteresis loops of soft iron, hardened steel and Alnico 5 (use the zoom). Which would you use for a transformer core, and which for a permanent magnet? Why?
  12. Magnetize hardened steel, set B = 0, and then press Demagnetize. What happens to the domains and to the remanent magnetization?
  13. Choose nickel with a field applied and raise the temperature past 627 K. What happens to the magnetization and to the domains? Cool it again with B = 0: does the magnetization come back?
  14. Gadolinium has a Curie temperature of 293 K. Compare its behaviour at 250 K and at 330 K.
  15. Compare the balance readings of a diamagnet, a paramagnet and a ferromagnet at B = 0.5 T. Roughly how many times stronger is the ferromagnetic force?

Types of Magnetism - Hands on Activity:

You need a strong neodymium magnet, two grapes, a drinking straw, a toothpick or thread, a steel paper clip, an iron nail, aluminium foil, a pencil, and a compass. Neodymium magnets can pinch fingers and are dangerous if swallowed; keep them away from phones and cards.

  1. Push a grape onto each end of the straw and balance the straw on the head of the toothpick (or hang it from a thread) so it can turn freely. Slowly bring the magnet close to one grape without touching it. What happens? Repeat with the other pole of the magnet.
  2. Repeat with small balls of aluminium foil instead of grapes. Compare.
  3. Hold the magnet near a single steel paper clip. Then try a stack of graphite shavings from a pencil on a sheet of paper. Compare the strength of the effects.
  4. Check that the paper clip does not attract a second clip. Stroke it about 20 times in one direction with one pole of the magnet. Can it now pick up a second clip? Use the compass to find its poles.
  5. Drop the magnetized paper clip on a hard floor several times, then test it again.
  6. Repeat step 4 with the iron nail. Remove the magnet and test the nail right away and again after a minute.
  1. Which way did the grapes move? What type of magnetism does this show, and which substance in the grapes is responsible?
  2. Why is the effect on the grapes so much weaker than the effect on the paper clip?
  3. Why did stroking the paper clip turn it into a magnet? Describe what happened to its domains.
  4. What did dropping the paper clip do to its magnetism? Explain using domains.
  5. Did the nail keep its magnetism as well as the paper clip? Is the nail magnetically soft or hard?
  6. Why does a magnet attract a paper clip whichever pole you use, but a magnet can either attract or repel a compass needle?
