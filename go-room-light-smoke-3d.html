This simulation follows the light of a laser or a flashlight in a dark room whose walls, floor and ceiling are matte. A matte surface reflects diffusely: each lit point sends light in all directions, so it can be seen from anywhere in the room and it faintly lights the rest of the room. The light reflected from one surface onto another follows

`E ~~ (rho E_0 A cos theta_e cos theta_r)/(pi r^2)`,

where `rho` is the fraction the surface reflects and `theta_e`, `theta_r` are the angles between the line joining the two surfaces and their normals.

Laser and flashlight. A laser beam hardly spreads, so in clean air it shows up only as a small bright dot where it lands. A flashlight beam spreads into a cone of half-angle `alpha`; at a distance `L` its spot is about `D ~~ 2 L tan alpha` across, so the same light covers a larger area: the spot grows dimmer, and since more of the room is lit, the diffuse light in the room becomes more even.

Visible and invisible light. The eye sees wavelengths from about 380 nm (violet) to 750 nm (red). A laser of shorter wavelength (ultraviolet) or longer (infrared) still carries energy and still lands on the wall, but leaves no visible dot at all. Many cameras do see near-infrared light, which is how you can check a TV remote control. Invisible laser beams are especially dangerous to the eye, because the eye gives no warning glare.

Smoke. In clean air light travels in a straight line and none of it reaches the eye unless it hits something, so the beam itself is invisible. Smoke particles scatter a little of the light in all directions; that scattered light reaches the eye from every point along the beam, which makes the beam visible. The light that is scattered (or absorbed) is lost from the beam, so its intensity falls with distance as

`I = I_0 e^(-sigma L)`,

where `sigma` (per metre) grows with the density of the smoke. The brightness of the beam seen from the side is proportional to `sigma`.

A smoke detector. A photoelectric smoke detector uses exactly this. Inside a dark chamber, a light source (usually an infrared LED) shines across the chamber into a light trap. A light sensor is placed at right angles to the beam, and a baffle stops the light from reaching it directly, so in clean air the sensor sees nothing. When smoke enters the chamber, its particles scatter some light toward the sensor; when the signal passes a threshold, the alarm sounds. The expected signal is proportional to the number of particles in the region where the beam and the sensor's view overlap -- that is, to the smoke density.
