The Earth and the Moon are drawn as seen from far above the North Pole. The Earth turns counterclockwise, and the Moon goes around the Earth counterclockwise too. Distances and water heights are not drawn to scale.

1. The pull on each piece. Think of the Earth as made of many small pieces of mass `dm`. The Moon pulls on each piece with a gravitational force

`dF = (G M_(moon) dm)/r^2`,

where `r` is the distance from the piece to the Moon. The piece on the near side is closer to the Moon, so it is pulled harder; the piece on the far side is pulled less. Pieces above and below the Earth–Moon line are pulled along slightly different directions, all pointing at the Moon. Because the force is not the same everywhere, the center of gravity, the average position of the gravitational force,

`x_(cg) = (F_(g1) x_1 + F_(g2) x_2 + ...)/(F_(g1) + F_(g2) + ...)`,

is not at the center of mass: it is a little closer to the Moon. The whole Earth accelerates toward the Moon as if every piece got the pull felt at the center. What is left over, each piece’s pull minus the pull at the center, is the tidal force. On the near side it points toward the Moon; on the far side it points away from the Moon (that piece is pulled less than the Earth as a whole, so it is left behind); on the sides it points slightly inward. The tidal force stretches the Earth along the Earth–Moon line. The water, free to move, piles up into two bulges: one facing the Moon and one on the far side. The size of the tidal force at the Earth’s surface is about

`a_(tidal) ~~ (2 G M_(moon) R)/d^3`,

so it falls off as the cube of the distance `d`, faster than gravity itself.

2. Tides through a day. The bulges stay lined up with the Moon while the Earth turns under them. A harbour therefore passes through two high tides and two low tides each day. Because the Moon also moves along its orbit in the same direction, the harbour needs a little more than a day, about 24 h 50 min, to face the Moon again: high tides come every 12 h 25 min.

3. Sun and Moon. The Sun also raises two bulges. The Sun is far more massive, but it is also far more distant, and the tidal force goes as `M//d^3`: the Sun’s bulges are only about 46% as large as the Moon’s. At new moon and full moon the Sun, the Earth and the Moon line up, the bulges add, and the tides are large: spring tides. At first and third quarter the bulges are at right angles and partly cancel: neap tides. This happens twice a month.

The heights shown are those of the “equilibrium tide”: about 0.36 m for the Moon’s bulge and 0.16 m for the Sun’s. Real tides along a coast are often several metres high, because the shape of the coast and the ocean basins amplify them.

4. The Moon’s same face. The Earth also raises tides on the Moon, and long ago it stretched the Moon into a slightly elongated shape (drawn much exaggerated). The Earth pulls harder on the Moon’s near end than on its far end. If the long axis is not lined up with the Earth, the two pulls are not along the same line: the center of gravity of the Moon is off the line through its center, and the Earth exerts a torque on the Moon that turns its long axis back toward the Earth. A Moon spinning faster or slower than it orbits is slowed down or sped up by this torque and by friction inside the flexing Moon, until it turns exactly once per orbit, every 27.3 days. Then the same side always faces the Earth: the Moon is tidally locked. Note that the locked Moon does spin: a Moon that did not spin at all would show us all of its sides during one orbit. The inset shows the Moon as seen from the Earth.

The same tidal effects act on the Earth: friction from the tides slowly lengthens the day (by about 2 milliseconds per century) and the Moon slowly moves away (about 3.8 cm per year).
