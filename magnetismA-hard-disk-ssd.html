How close is 3 nm?

The head flies on a cushion of air dragged along by the spinning platter, a few nanometres above it. A smoke particle (about 1000 nm), a fingerprint (about 15 000 nm) or a hair (about 80 000 nm) is a mountain at that scale: if the head hits one, or touches the surface (a head crash), it scrapes away the magnetic coating and the data on it. That is why drives are assembled in clean rooms and sealed, some filled with helium, which lets the platters spin more smoothly.

Why a controller is needed

A flash page can be written only once until its whole block is erased, and each erase wears the cells (about 1000 to 100 000 times, depending on the bits per cell). So the controller never overwrites in place: it writes the new version elsewhere, keeps a map from the computer's block numbers to the pages, collects stale pages into blocks it can erase, and spreads the erases evenly (wear levelling). The extra writes this needs are the write amplification.

Inside a hard drive. One or more rigid platters (aluminium or glass, coated with a magnetic film on both faces) spin together on a spindle at a constant 5400 to 15 000 revolutions per minute. Each face has its own read/write head, at the tip of an arm; all the arms pivot together about one axis, swung by a voice-coil actuator (a coil between permanent magnets: the force on a current in a magnetic field). The heads fly a few nanometres above the surface on a cushion of air dragged by the platter; touching the surface (a head crash) destroys the data there, so drives are sealed, and the heads are parked on a ramp before the platters stop. The data lie on concentric tracks, cut into sectors; the outer tracks, longer, hold more sectors. Reading a sector takes a seek (the arm accelerates, then brakes), a short settling time, a wait for the sector to come round (on average half a turn) and the read itself: in all several milliseconds, almost all of it mechanical.

Writing and reading a disk. A hard disk stores bits in a thin magnetic film made of tiny grains, each a few nanometres across, magnetized one way or the other. The write head is an electromagnet: a current in its coil makes a strong field under its narrow pole, which sets the grains passing beneath it. Reversing the current reverses the magnetization written from then on. Drives store a 1 as a reversal (a transition) and a 0 as no reversal. The read sensor, a magnetoresistive sensor (GMR or TMR) whose resistance depends on the field, flies a few nanometres above the disk and picks up the field of the grains passing under it. In perpendicular recording (used since about 2005) the grains point up or down and the signal follows the magnetization; in the older longitudinal recording they lie along the track, and the signal is a pulse at each transition. Because the grains have random sizes, transitions are jagged; because the sensor is not infinitely close, neighbouring bits blur together. Both limit how short a bit can be.

Keeping the bits. More grains per bit give a cleaner signal, so denser disks need smaller grains. But a grain's magnetization is held in place by an energy barrier `K_u V` (the anisotropy energy density times the volume), and thermal energy `k_B T` keeps trying to flip it. A grain flips on its own after a time

`tau = tau_0 e^(K_u V // k_B T)`, with `tau_0 ~~ 1 ns`.

Keeping data for ten years needs `K_u V // k_B T` of about 50 or more. Smaller grains need a "harder" material (larger `K_u`), but its anisotropy field `mu_0 H_k = 2 K_u // M_s` then exceeds what a write head can make (about 2 T). Signal, stability and writability cannot all be satisfied: the recording trilemma. Heat-assisted magnetic recording (HAMR) escapes it by heating the grains with a laser for an instant while writing, which lowers `K_u` and so `H_k`; they cool down stable.

The solid-state drive. A flash memory cell is a transistor with an extra, isolated electrode, the floating gate, buried in insulating oxide. A high voltage on the control gate pulls electrons from the channel through the thin tunnel oxide by quantum tunnelling (Fowler-Nordheim tunnelling): the current density

`J = A E^2 e^(-B // E)`

rises steeply with the field `E` in the oxide. The trapped electrons push the transistor's threshold voltage up by `Delta V_t = N e // C`, about 40 mV per electron here; they stay for years, since without a high field there is almost no tunnelling. Erasing pulls them out the same way. Reading applies a voltage between the levels and asks whether the transistor conducts. Storing 2, 3 or 4 bits in one cell (MLC, TLC, QLC) means telling apart 4, 8 or 16 threshold levels. Every program and erase damages the oxide a little: the levels broaden, and over time they lose charge and drift down, until neighbouring levels overlap and bits are misread. Error-correcting codes fix a few errors; more levels per cell leave less room, which is why QLC drives wear out sooner.

Two ways put electrons on the floating gate. In NAND flash (SSDs, memory cards), a high voltage on the control gate makes them tunnel through the oxide. In NOR flash, 12 V on the drain speeds electrons along the channel, and near the drain some gain enough energy to jump over the oxide barrier (hot-electron injection). Both are erased by tunnelling.

Inside an SSD. An SSD has no moving parts: a controller (a small processor), often a DRAM cache, and NAND flash chips, whose cells are stacked in layers (3D NAND). Flash is written by pages (about 16 kB) but erased only by whole blocks (several MB), and a page cannot be rewritten before its block is erased. So the controller writes each new version elsewhere and marks the old page stale; deleting a file only marks its pages stale (the TRIM command). Garbage collection copies the valid pages of a block elsewhere and erases it. Since each block survives only so many erases, the controller spreads them (wear levelling), even moving data that never changes. These extra writes make the flash write more than the computer asked: the write amplification.
