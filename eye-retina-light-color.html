This simulation shows that what color we see depends not only on the light that reaches the eye, but also on where its image falls on the retina, how large the image is, and how much light there is.

The retina has two kinds of light detectors. About 6 million cones work in good light and give us color and sharp detail; about 120 million rods work in dim light, are far more sensitive, but cannot tell colors apart. They are not spread evenly. The fovea, in the middle of the retina, is packed with cones -- about 200,000 per square millimetre -- and has no rods at all; away from it the cones thin out quickly and the rods take over, most densely about 20 degrees out. The very center of the fovea also lacks the blue-sensitive S cones. About 15 degrees toward the nose there are no receptors at all: the blind spot.

Where the image falls. We see sharply and in full color only near the center of gaze, which is why we move our eyes to look at things. A small colored object seen out of the corner of the eye looks greyish and blurred; a large one keeps more of its color. A tiny blue dot looked at directly loses its blueness, because its image falls where there are no S cones.

How much light. In daylight (photopic vision, above a few candela per square metre) only the cones work. At night (scotopic vision, below about a thousandth of a candela per square metre) only the rods work: the world turns grey, and since the fovea has no rods, a faint star looked at directly disappears -- astronomers look slightly to one side of it. In between, at dusk (mesopic vision), both work.

The cones are most sensitive to light of about 555 nm, the rods to about 507 nm. As the light fades and the rods take over, blue and green things look relatively brighter and red things darker: a red rose that looks brighter than a blue flower at noon looks darker than it at dusk. This is the Purkinje shift, after the physiologist who noticed it in his garden. The relative brightness of each flower to the rods is computed from its color with the scotopic luminance formula

`Y' ~~ 1.182 Y + 0.362 Z - 0.805 X`,

in terms of the color's tristimulus values `X`, `Y`, `Z` (Larson, Rushmeier and Piatko, 1997).

The densities follow the measurements of Curcio and colleagues (1990), in simplified form. The appearance is a conceptual model: it shows the trends -- loss of color and detail away from the center, grey night vision, the rod-free fovea, the Purkinje shift -- rather than an exact rendering of perception.
