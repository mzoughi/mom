Readout

This simulation shows how refraction at a water surface changes what we see. Light from an object under water bends as it leaves the water, following Snell's law,

`n_w sin theta_w = n_a sin theta_a`,

so the rays that reach the eye seem to come from a point closer to the surface: the image. The simulation finds the ray from the object to the eye by Fermat's principle (light takes the path of least time), and locates the image where neighbouring rays, traced backwards, meet.

Apparent depth. Looking straight down, an object at depth `d` appears at depth `d/n`, three-quarters of its depth in water. Looking at a slant, the image rises further and shifts toward the eye: there is no single image position, and a long object looks bent and squeezed. The graph compares the apparent depth with `d/n` at every viewing angle. Drag the object, the eye, or draw your own shape. Put the eye underwater, and an object in the air looks higher than it is.

The pool. A clown stands in a pool; the view from the eye is computed by tracing a refracted ray in three dimensions for every pixel. The submerged part looks shorter, shifted and wider at the bottom, like legs in a swimming pool. Compare with the empty pool.

Snell's window. Seen from under water, light from the whole sky arrives within a cone of half-angle `theta_c = arcsin(1/n)`, 48.8° for water: the sky is squeezed into a bright circle, and everything near the horizon crowds at its rim. Beyond the critical angle the surface is a mirror (total internal reflection) showing the pool floor.
