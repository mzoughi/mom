A playground spinner turns freely about a vertical axis. With no torque from outside (no push, no friction), the total angular momentum about that axis stays the same, whatever the children do on it.

Moving on the spinner. The spinner is a disk, `I = 1/2 M R^2`, and each child adds `m r^2`. When a child walks toward the centre, `r` and so the total `I` decrease, and the spinner turns faster: `I_1 omega_1 = I_2 omega_2`. Walking back out slows it down. The kinetic energy changes too: the child does work walking in, against the push that keeps them on the circle.

Jumping on. A child running at speed `v` along a line at a distance `d` from the axis has an angular momentum `m v d` about it. Running along the rim in the direction it turns, that adds to the spinner's; running against it, it subtracts (enough of it can stop or reverse the spinner); running straight at the centre, `d = 0`, it adds nothing. Once on: `(I_s omega_0 + m v d) = (I_s + m R^2) omega`. Kinetic energy is lost, as in any collision where two things stick together.

Jumping off. A child who simply steps off keeps the rim's velocity and moves off along a straight line, tangent to the circle: the spinner keeps turning at the same rate. A child who pushes off backward so as to land standing still leaves with no angular momentum: the spinner keeps all of it, and turns faster.
