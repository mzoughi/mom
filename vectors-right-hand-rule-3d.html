The Right-Hand Rule: Coordinate Frames, Cross Products and Curls

This simulation shows the right-hand rule in three dimensions, with a hand you can turn around by dragging.

1. The right-hand frame. Physics uses right-handed coordinate systems. Point the thumb of your right hand along `x`, the index finger along `y`, and bend the middle finger at right angles to both: it points along `z`. The unit vectors then obey

`hat x xx hat y = hat z`,   `hat y xx hat z = hat x`,   `hat z xx hat x = hat y`,

and reversing the order of any product changes its sign. Doing the same with the left hand makes the middle finger point the other way: a left-handed frame, its mirror image. No rotation turns one into the other, just as no rotation turns a right glove into a left one.

2. The cross product. The cross product of two vectors is a vector perpendicular to both,

`vec a xx vec b = (a_y b_z - a_z b_y, a_z b_x - a_x b_z, a_x b_y - a_y b_x)`,   `|vec a xx vec b| = a b sin theta`,

whose length is the area of the parallelogram the two vectors span. Its direction comes from the same hand: thumb along `vec a`, index finger along `vec b` (or the part of `vec b` perpendicular to `vec a`), and the middle finger gives `vec a xx vec b`. Then `vec b xx vec a = -vec a xx vec b`.

3. The curl rule. For things that turn, curl the fingers of the right hand in the direction of rotation: the thumb points along the angular velocity `vec omega`, and so along the angular momentum `vec L = I vec omega`. The same rule links a current to the magnetic field it makes: with the thumb along the current in a straight wire, the fingers curl in the direction of the field lines circling the wire. The curl rule is the cross-product rule in disguise: for a point on a turning object, `vec v = vec omega xx vec r`.

4. The quiz. Coordinate axes are shown in a random orientation, sometimes mirrored. Decide whether they form a right-handed or a left-handed frame: try to line up your right hand's thumb, index and middle finger with `x`, `y` and `z`.
