The forces. On a wheel on an incline of angle `theta` act its weight (split into `mg sin theta` down the slope and `mg cos theta` into it), the normal force `N = mg cos theta`, and friction `f` at the contact point, up the slope. Gravity acts at the centre, so it exerts no torque about it; only friction does, `tau = f R`. Without friction the wheel would slide down without turning.

Rolling without slipping. For the contact point to stay at rest, the wheel must speed up its spin as fast as it speeds up: `a = alpha R`. With `I = c m R^2`, Newton's laws (`m a = m g sin theta - f`, `I alpha = f R`) give

`a = (g sin theta)/(1 + c)` and `f = m g sin theta * c/(1 + c)`.

That friction is static (the contact point does not move), and static friction can be at most `mu_s N = mu_s m g cos theta`. So the wheel rolls only if `tan theta <= mu_s (1 + c)/c`. Beyond that critical angle it slips: kinetic friction `mu_k m g cos theta` acts, the centre accelerates at `g (sin theta - mu_k cos theta)`, and the spin cannot keep up.

Energy. Rolling without slipping, static friction does no work: the potential energy lost becomes kinetic energy of translation and of rotation, in the ratio 1 to `c`, so `v = sqrt(2 g h/(1 + c))`. Slipping, kinetic friction turns some of it into heat. A frictionless block (no rotation) arrives first; among the rolling shapes, the smaller `c`, the faster.
