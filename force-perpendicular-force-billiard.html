Kicks and Sideways Forces: From a Billiard Ball to a Charge in a Magnetic Field

This simulation shows what a force does to a moving object depending on its direction, using a billiard ball on a frictionless table and, at the end, a charged particle in a magnetic field.

1. A kick. With no force on it, the ball moves in a straight line at constant velocity (Newton's first law); the ghost images, taken at equal time intervals, are equally spaced along a line. A kick from the cue is a large force acting for a very short time. It gives the ball an impulse `vec J`, changing its velocity by

`Delta vec v = vec J / m`.

A kick along the motion speeds the ball up; against it, slows it down; sideways, it turns the ball. A kick at exactly 90 degrees also makes the ball a little faster, since the new velocity is the vector sum: `|vec v'| = sqrt(v^2 + (Delta v)^2)`. To turn the velocity by an angle `delta` without changing its length, the kick must point slightly backward, toward the centre of the turn, at `90^@ + delta/2` from the velocity, with `Delta v = 2 v sin(delta/2)`. Many such kicks in a row bend the path into a polygon around a circle at constant speed. The smaller and more frequent the kicks, the closer the two directions become -- in the limit of a continuous force, perpendicular and toward the centre are the same.

2. A force always sideways. A force that is always perpendicular to the velocity never speeds the ball up or slows it down -- it does no work -- it only turns it. The ball moves in a circle at constant speed. The force supplies the centripetal acceleration,

`F = m v^2 / r`,   so   `r = m v^2 / F`,   `T = (2 pi r)/v = (2 pi m v)/F`.

A force of the same size pointing in a fixed direction is different: it changes the speed as well as the direction, and the path becomes a parabola.

3. A charge in a magnetic field. A magnetic field gives a moving charge exactly this kind of force. The magnetic force

`vec F = q vec v xx vec B`

is always perpendicular to the velocity, so the charge moves in a circle at constant speed, with `q v B = m v^2/r`:

`r = (m v)/(|q| B)`,   `T = (2 pi m)/(|q| B)`.

The period does not depend on the speed: faster particles move on larger circles and take the same time to go around. This is the principle of the cyclotron. Positive and negative charges turn in opposite directions. If the velocity has a component along the field, that component is not affected, and the circle stretches into a helix. Tracks like these, curling in a magnetic field, are how physicists identify particles in bubble chambers and detectors.
