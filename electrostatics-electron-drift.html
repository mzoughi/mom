This simulation shows the free electrons inside a conductor. Without an electric field, the electrons move fast but in random directions, colliding with the ions of the metal, so on average they go nowhere and there is no current.

When a field `vec E` is applied, each electron (charge `-e`) is pushed opposite to the field between collisions. On top of the random thermal motion, the electrons then drift slowly against the field with the drift velocity

`v_d = (e E tau)/m`,

where `tau` is the average time between collisions. The current that results through a wire of cross-section `A`, with `n` free electrons per unit volume, is

`I = n e A v_d`.

The drift velocity is tiny -- typically a fraction of a millimetre per second -- even though the random speeds are enormous. Electrons leave one end of the conductor and enter at the other, so the conductor stays neutral while the current flows.

This simulation is useful for studying the microscopic picture of electric current, drift velocity, and the difference between random motion and a net flow.
