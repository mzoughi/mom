Two-Ball Collisions: Elastic and Inelastic

This simulation shows two balls colliding on a frictionless table, treated as point particles that do not spin. During the collision each ball pushes the other with an equal and opposite force (Newton's third law), along the line joining their centres. So whatever momentum one ball gains, the other loses: the total momentum of the two balls is conserved,

`m_1 vec v_1 + m_2 vec v_2 = m_1 vec v_1' + m_2 vec v_2'`.

Momentum is a vector: both its components are conserved, which the diagram of momentum arrows shows. The centre of mass, `vec v_(cm) = (m_1 vec v_1 + m_2 vec v_2)/(m_1 + m_2)`, moves straight on at constant velocity, collision or not.

Elastic collisions also conserve kinetic energy, `1/2 m_1 v_1^2 + 1/2 m_2 v_2^2`. In a head-on elastic collision between equal masses the balls simply exchange velocities: a moving ball stops dead and the one it hits moves off at its speed. In a glancing elastic collision between equal masses, with one ball at rest, the two balls always move off at right angles to each other.

Inelastic collisions turn some kinetic energy into heat, sound and deformation. How much is set by the coefficient of restitution `e`, the ratio of the speeds at which the balls separate and approach along the line of impact: `e = 1` is elastic, `e = 0` means they do not bounce apart along that line. In a perfectly inelastic collision the balls stick together and move off with the velocity of the centre of mass,

`vec v' = (m_1 vec v_1 + m_2 vec v_2)/(m_1 + m_2)`,

which loses the most kinetic energy that momentum conservation allows; when a ball of mass `m_1` hits one of mass `m_2` at rest, a fraction `m_2/(m_1 + m_2)` of the kinetic energy is lost.

In the simulation, the impulse along the line of impact `hat n` is `J = -(1 + e) (vec v_"rel" * hat n)/(1/m_1 + 1/m_2)`; the impact parameter `b` (0 for head-on) sets how far off-centre the balls meet. The run stops when a ball reaches a cushion, since a cushion would change the momentum.
