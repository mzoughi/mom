Seeing in 3D: Two Eyes, 3D Movies, One Eye, and the Brain

Each eye forms a flat image on its retina, yet we see a world in depth. The depth is not in either image: the brain builds it, from several kinds of clues. This simulation explores them in four parts.

1. Two eyes. The eyes are about 6.3 cm apart, so each sees the world from a slightly different place. A near object and a far one line up differently in the two images; the difference in their angles is the binocular disparity. For an eye separation `b` and objects at distances `d_1` and `d_2`,

`delta ~~ b (1/d_1 - 1/d_2)`  (in radians).

The brain fuses the two images and reads the disparity as depth: this is stereopsis. It is remarkably sensitive -- a disparity of about 20 seconds of arc can be detected -- so at arm's length a depth difference of a millimetre or two can be seen. But the disparity shrinks with the square of the distance, so beyond a few hundred metres stereopsis no longer helps.

2. 3D movies show each eye its own picture, filmed from two positions about an eye's width apart. The trick is to send the left picture only to the left eye and the right one only to the right eye. Red-cyan glasses (anaglyph) let each eye see only one color, at the cost of the colors. Polarized glasses use light polarized two ways; with linear polarizers, tilting the head lets the other picture leak through, by Malus's law a fraction `sin^2 theta` of it, so cinemas use circular polarization, which does not care about tilt. Shutter glasses darken each eye in turn, in step with a screen that alternates the pictures many times a second. Wavelength-multiplex glasses (such as Dolby 3D) give each eye its own slightly different red, green and blue, and filter out the other set. Without glasses, both eyes see both pictures: a double image.

Filmmakers rarely use the eyes' own geometry. In a cinema the eyes converge on the screen while staying focused on it, so large disparities are uncomfortable; the separation of an object's two pictures on the screen grows with the size of the screen; and a background separated by more than the 6.3 cm between the eyes would force the eyes to turn outward. For a rig converged at a distance `D_c`, with cameras `t` apart and a field of view `2 alpha`, an object at distance `Z` appears on the screen separated by a fraction

`P = (t (1/D_c - 1/Z))/(2 tan alpha)`

of the screen's width -- positive (uncrossed) behind the screen, negative (crossed) in front of it. Cinematographers therefore keep `P` within a depth budget of a few percent and often put the cameras only 1 to 4 cm apart.

Lenticular prints (like those from the four-lens Nimslo camera) need no glasses. The picture is cut into thin strips, one from each view, interlaced behind a sheet of tiny cylindrical lenses. Each lens shows each eye a different strip, so from any position the two eyes see two neighbouring views. Moving the head brings in other pairs, and the picture seems to turn; at the edge of the viewing zone the eyes get the views in the wrong order and the depth turns inside out. Each view fills a zone at the eyes about `(p/N) n D/t` wide, for a lens pitch `p`, `N` views, sheet thickness `t`, index `n` and viewing distance `D`; the views are taken closer together than the eyes so that neighbouring views make a comfortable pair.

3. One eye. People with one eye, or anyone with one eye closed, still see depth, from monocular cues: farther things look smaller (relative size), nearer things hide farther ones (occlusion), parallel lines converge (linear perspective), farther things sit higher toward the horizon, look hazier, and cast shadows that tie them to the ground. Moving the head is a powerful cue: near things slide across the view faster than far ones (motion parallax).

4. The brain decides. All these clues can be ambiguous, and the brain resolves them with assumptions. It assumes light comes from above, so the same shaded disc looks like a bump or a dent depending on which way up it is. A line drawing of a cube can be seen two ways, and perception flips between them. In a corridor drawn in perspective, two identical figures look very different in size, because the brain corrects for the distance it believes each is at. What we see is the brain's best guess about the world, not a copy of the images on the retina.
