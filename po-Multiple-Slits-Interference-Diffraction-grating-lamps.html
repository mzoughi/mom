This simulation turns the diffraction grating into a spectroscope. Each gas-discharge lamp emits light only at certain wavelengths, the spectral lines of its atoms, and a grating sends each wavelength in its own direction,

`d sin theta = n lambda`,   `n = 0, +-1, +-2, ...`

so each line appears on the screen, in every order, at its own place. The lamps show their real visible lines (from 400 to 700 nm, from the NIST atomic spectra database), with a typical relative brightness; in a real tube the brightness depends on the pressure and the current. Hydrogen shows the four Balmer lines; neon its forest of red and orange lines; sodium its yellow doublet (589.0 and 589.6 nm, too close to separate here); mercury its violet, blue, green and yellow lines. Lasers give a single line, and white light a continuous spectrum. The zeroth order mixes all the lines: it has the lamp's own colour.

To measure a wavelength, move the marker onto a line and read the angle: for a first-order line `lambda = d sin theta`, for a second-order line `lambda = (d sin theta)/2`. The readout does this arithmetic for both orders. Moving the screen farther away spreads the lines apart, so that close lines can be told apart.
