This simulation shows how an optical fiber guides light. A step-index fiber has a glass core of refractive index `n_1` surrounded by a cladding of slightly lower index `n_2`. Light that enters the core keeps bouncing off the boundary between the core and the cladding, and so it follows the fiber, even around bends.

When the light enters the end of the fiber from the outside medium (index `n_0`), it is refracted:

`n_0 sin theta_0 = n_1 sin theta_1`.

Inside the core the ray meets the core-cladding boundary at the angle `phi = 90^@ - theta_1` from the normal. If `phi` is larger than the critical angle `phi_c`, given by

`sin phi_c = n_2/n_1`,

the light is totally reflected and none of it is lost. If `phi` is smaller, only part of the light is reflected at each bounce (as given by the Fresnel equations), and the rest leaks into the cladding, where the coating absorbs it. After a few bounces, almost nothing is left.

Combining the two conditions gives the largest launch angle that is still guided, the acceptance angle `theta_"max"`. It is described by the numerical aperture

`"NA" = n_0 sin theta_"max" = sqrt(n_1^2 - n_2^2)`.

Light that arrives within this acceptance cone is guided; light that arrives more steeply escapes. When `n_1^2 - n_2^2` is larger than `n_0^2`, every launch angle is guided.

The cladding is what protects the guiding. A bare glass rod in air also guides light, but water, dirt or a finger touching it raises the index outside the glass, so the light leaks out where it touches. With a cladding, the guiding happens at the core-cladding boundary, which nothing outside can touch.

When the fiber is bent, the rays meet the outer wall of the bend at a smaller angle `phi`. In a gentle bend this changes little; in a tight bend, rays that were guided in the straight part meet the outer wall below the critical angle and leak out. This is why fibers have a minimum bend radius. (The fiber here is drawn far wider than a real one, so even its gentlest bend, a few core widths across, would be extremely tight for a real fiber, whose core is only about 0.05 mm wide.)

Rays that enter at different angles travel different distances: the steepest guided ray zig-zags the most and arrives last. A short pulse of light therefore spreads out as it travels. For a step-index fiber of length `L`, the difference in travel time between the straight ray and the steepest guided ray is

`Delta t = (L n_1)/c (n_1/n_2 - 1)`,

where `c` is the speed of light in vacuum. This modal dispersion limits how close together the pulses carrying data can be sent. The pulse graph shows a short pulse before and after the chosen length of fiber, using the paths of the rays traced in the side view.

Users can change the indices of the core and the cladding, the outside medium, the launch angle and the point of entry, send a single ray or a fan of rays, bend the fiber with a chosen radius, and choose the fiber length for the pulse. Drag the laser up or down in the side view to tilt the beam.

This simulation is useful for studying total internal reflection, the critical angle, the acceptance angle and numerical aperture of a fiber, the role of the cladding, bending losses, and modal dispersion.
