The slab has two parallel faces (top interface at y=0, bottom at y=−thickness), both bordering the SAME outside medium n₁. For a parallel-faced slab this guarantees the exit ray is always parallel to the incident ray (just laterally shifted) and that total internal reflection, when it happens, can only happen at the FIRST interface — verified before building this. Reflection/transmission amplitudes (rs, rp, ts, tp) use the standard Fresnel equations, applied once at each interface; multiple internal reflections inside the slab are not tracked, only the primary transmitted path, which keeps the scene legible. s-polarization (φ=0°) is always perpendicular to the plane of incidence; p-polarization (φ=90°) lies within it — since s and p reflect/transmit differently, a mixed (φ≠0,90°) incident wave generally comes out a DIFFERENT mix after reflecting or refracting. Color (Visible band only) is tied to the source frequency and never changes crossing an interface. The faint continuous ribbon along each ray (toggle with "Show wave envelope") traces the full oscillation, not just the sampled arrows — it's the most direct way to see whether a crest in the incident wave arrives at the interface and continues as a crest (r>0, no flip) or a trough (r<0, a π phase flip) in the reflected or refracted wave. One genuinely confusing but standard fact worth knowing: by the usual convention for writing the Fresnel equations, rs and rp have OPPOSITE signs from each other at near-normal incidence, even though s and p are physically indistinguishable there — so with a mixed polarization (φ away from 0° or 90°) at small θ₁, it's normal and correct for the s-part and p-part of the reflected envelope to flip oppositely. This was verified against the actual Maxwell boundary condition (tangential E continuous across the interface at all times) before building this, independent of any textbook sign convention. The dots mark exactly where each ray crosses an interface. "Show static field" freezes incident, reflected, and refracted at phase=0 (their own true sign and magnitude, not animating) so you can read their relative orientation directly, without tracking the animation at all — the most direct tool here if the moving envelope is still hard to follow. Color-coding gives each of the four rays its own fixed color (overriding the Visible-band wavelength tint, since the point is telling them apart, not showing their true color). "Show normal" draws the standard dashed reference line that θ₁, θ₂, and the reflection angle are all measured from, at each interface. "Show plane of incidence" shades the flat z=0 plane that every ray in this scene already lies in — framed around the first interface, since that's where incidence, reflection, and the start of refraction all happen together.
  1. With n₁=1.0 and n₂=1.5, slide θ₁ from 0° up. Does the refracted ray inside the slab bend toward or away from the normal compared to the incident ray?
  2. Now set n₁=1.5 and n₂=1.0 (swap them) and increase θ₁. At some angle the refracted and exit rays disappear entirely. What is this called, and can you find the angle where it first happens?
  3. Set φ=90° (pure p) and slide θ₁ until the reflected ray's arrows shrink to nothing. What is this angle called, and does it depend on n₁,n₂?
  4. At that same angle, switch to φ=0° (pure s). Does the reflected ray vanish now too?
  5. Set φ=45° (a mix of s and p) at the Brewster angle you just found. Is the reflected ray still at 45° polarization, or has its mix changed? What does that tell you about why polarized sunglasses work?
  6. Turn on both Es and Ep. Away from Brewster's angle, compare their sizes in the incident ray to their sizes in the reflected ray. Do Es and Ep shrink by the same factor on reflection, or different factors?
  7. Increase the slab thickness. What changes about the exit ray's position relative to where the incident ray would have continued if there were no slab at all?
  8. Does changing θ₁ or n₁,n₂ ever change the wave's frequency f? What does change?
  9. Use the four ray-visibility checkboxes to show only the incident and refracted (in-slab) rays, hiding the rest. With just those two, is it easier to see the bend at the first interface described by Snell's law?
  10. Set n₁=1.0, n₂=1.5, θ₁ small (near 0°), φ=45°, and turn on the wave envelope. Pause and step frame by frame. Does the Es part of the reflected envelope line up crest-to-crest with the incident, or crest-to-trough? Is it the same answer for Ep?
  11. Repeat the same setup, but now turn on "Show static field" instead (no need to pause or step). Do the frozen incident and reflected arrows confirm what you found in the previous question?
  12. Turn on both "Show normal" and "Show plane of incidence." Rotate the view until you're looking straight along the z-axis (edge-on to the plane of incidence, so it collapses to a line). Can you now directly read off θ₁ and θ₂ as the angles between each ray and the dashed normal?
