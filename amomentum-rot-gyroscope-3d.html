THE GYROSCOPE AND THE STICK
The gyroscope
The stick

A toy gyroscope is a heavy rotor (a disk) that spins on an axle inside a light frame, here two crossed hoops. The rotor turns on bearings, so the frame does not have to spin with it. A stick can strike the gyroscope anywhere, in any direction: you choose the point along the axle, the distance off the axle, the direction of the push and its strength. After each strike the stick pulls back out of view.

1. Floating free. In the space station there is no gravity to speak of, so nothing pushes on the gyroscope except the stick. A short kick delivers an impulse `vec J` at a point `vec r` from the centre of mass. It changes the momentum and the angular momentum:

`Delta vec p = m Delta vec v_(cm) = vec J`,   `Delta vec L = vec r xx vec J`.

A push through the centre of mass gives no torque: the gyroscope drifts away without turning. A push at the end of the axle also turns it. If the rotor is not spinning, `vec L` comes only from the kick, and the gyroscope tumbles end over end, with `omega = Delta L // I_(perp)`. If the rotor spins, the gyroscope already carries a large angular momentum along its axle, `L = I_s omega_s`. The same kick only adds a small `Delta vec L` to it, so `vec L` turns by a small angle,

`Delta theta ~~ (Delta L_(perp))/(I_s omega_s)`,

and with no torque afterwards `vec L` stays fixed. The axle wobbles quickly in a narrow cone around the new direction of `vec L` (a free nutation) but keeps nearly its old direction. The faster the spin, the smaller the cone. A stick that catches the rim of the rotor at right angles to the axle changes the spin itself. Choosing a new spin starts the situation again: the gyroscope floats at rest in its starting position. Floating free, it touches nothing, so there is no friction.

2. On a stand, with gravity. The lower end of the axle rests on a pivot at the top of a post. Gravity acts at the centre of mass, a distance `ell` from the pivot, and exerts a torque about the pivot,

`vec tau = vec r_(cm) xx m vec g`,   `tau = m g ell sin theta`,

which is horizontal and at right angles to the axle. Each time this tab is opened, or the spin is changed, the gyroscope starts the usual way: spinning, almost upright (0.5° off the vertical), with just the right sideways speed. The torque of gravity, `d vec L = vec tau dt`, only turns `vec L` sideways: the axle swings around the vertical, the precession, at the rate

`Omega ~~ (m g ell)/(I_s omega_s)`.

Near upright, a fast spin keeps the gyroscope up: it stays up only while `I_s omega_s > 2 sqrt(I_(perp) m g ell)`, here about 35 rev/s. Friction (air drag and the bearings) slowly takes the spin away. Once the spin falls below that value, the upright position becomes unstable: the circle traced by the tip widens, faster and faster, until the gyroscope falls against the post. Friction at the pivot also exerts a torque that opposes the precession, `tau_f = -c Omega`, about the vertical. It lowers the vertical part of `vec L`, so a tilted gyroscope drops further. Two sliders set these frictions; with both at zero the circle keeps its size. With no spin, nothing resists the torque of gravity and the gyroscope simply topples. A kick at the top end can make it speed up, slow down, rise or drop.

The model: rotor 100 g, radius 3.0 cm; frame 50 g, radius 3.6 cm; axle 9 cm long, so `ell` = 4.5 cm. The equations of rigid-body motion are solved numerically. The coloured sectors on the rotor show its rotation. When the rotor turns too fast for the screen to follow, the sectors turn at a slower, readable rate in the correct direction, and a curved arrow appears on the rotor as a reminder. Collisions with the post are modeled only as a bump that stops the fall.
