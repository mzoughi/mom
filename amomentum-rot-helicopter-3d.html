The Helicopter: Force, Torque and Angular Momentum

THE HELICOPTER
Main rotor: lift and forward motion
Tail rotor: torque and turning

A small helicopter (mass 1200 kg) sits on a landing pad. Its main rotor turns counterclockwise when seen from above. A small tail rotor, at the end of the tail, pushes the tail sideways. The SIM shows only the main ideas: forces, torques and angular momentum.

1. Lift: forces. The spinning blades push air down, so the air pushes the rotor up. This thrust grows with the square of the rotor speed, `T = k omega^2`. Newton's second law for the helicopter is

`vec T + vec W + vec D = m vec a`,

where `W = m g` is the weight and `vec D` is air resistance. When `T > W` the helicopter speeds up upward and climbs; air resistance then limits its climbing speed. When `T = W` the net force is zero: it hovers (or keeps a steady vertical speed). Here hovering needs about 400 rpm.

2. Torque and angular momentum. The engine exerts a torque on the rotor to keep it turning against the drag of the air. By Newton's third law, the rotor exerts an equal and opposite torque on the body. Without anything else, the body would turn the opposite way to the rotor: the angular momentum given to the rotor, `L_r = I_r omega_r`, comes with an opposite angular momentum of the body. The tail rotor solves this. Its sideways force `F_t`, at a distance `d` behind the main rotor, gives a torque about the vertical axis,

`tau_t = F_t d`,

which balances the reaction torque: `tau_t = tau_(engine)`. Then the net torque on the body is zero and it keeps its heading. Pushing a little more or a little less with the tail rotor (the pedals) leaves a net torque, and the helicopter turns one way or the other. Switch off the tail rotor to see the body spin, opposite to the rotor.

3. Forward flight. To move forward, the pilot tilts the main rotor (and with it the helicopter) forward by an angle `alpha`. The thrust then has a forward part and an upward part:

`T_(forward) = T sin alpha`,   `T_(up) = T cos alpha`.

The forward part speeds the helicopter up until air resistance balances it. The upward part is now smaller than `T`, so to stay level the rotor must turn a little faster, until `T cos alpha = W`. Tilting back slows the helicopter down.

The numbers: `k` = 6.7 N·s², the rotor's moment of inertia 500 kg·m², the body's 2000 kg·m², `d` = 7.2 m. The drawn blades turn more slowly than the real ones, in the correct direction, so the rotation stays visible; a curved arrow over the rotor then marks the sense of rotation.
