This simulation investigates static and kinetic friction by measuring the force required to pull a block across a surface. A force probe applies an increasing force to the block while the simulation records force, velocity, and time data. The results are used to determine the coefficients of static friction and kinetic friction for different materials and conditions.

The simulation displays a block resting on an adjustable surface. Users can control the applied pulling force, block mass, contact area, surface material combination, and track angle. The angle can be adjusted to investigate friction on both horizontal and inclined surfaces.

As the applied force increases, the block initially remains at rest because static friction matches the applied force. The force reaches a maximum value at the point where the block begins to move. This maximum frictional force is used to determine the coefficient of static friction.

Once the block is moving, the frictional force decreases to the kinetic-friction value. The simulation then records the force required to keep the block moving across the surface. The coefficient of kinetic friction is calculated from this average force.

A live-readings panel continuously displays the measured force, block velocity, elapsed time, and current motion status. These values update throughout the experiment, allowing users to observe the transition between static and kinetic friction.

The simulation automatically records force-versus-time data and displays the results on a graph. The graph can show the entire run, only the static-friction region, or only the kinetic-friction region. The static-friction region typically shows a steadily increasing force until breakaway, while the kinetic-friction region displays an approximately constant force while the block is moving.

Statistical analysis tools calculate quantities such as the minimum force, maximum force, mean force, standard deviation, and number of samples collected within the selected region. These values are used to determine the measured coefficients of static and kinetic friction.

Each completed trial is automatically stored in a run-data table. The table records the block mass, selected surface, incline angle, contact area, applied push force, normal force, maximum static-friction force, kinetic-friction force, and the measured values of the static and kinetic friction coefficients.

The analysis section allows users to compare measured friction coefficients with other experimental parameters. Data can be plotted against variables such as mass, contact area, incline angle, or surface type. Linear fits, averages, and standard deviations can be calculated to investigate how friction depends on different factors.

The simulation includes multiple surface combinations with different friction coefficients and an adjustable track angle. This allows users to test predictions from the friction models Fs,max = μsN and Fk = μkN, and to explore how gravity and the normal force influence frictional behavior.

Experimental data can be downloaded as a CSV file or copied directly from the data table for further analysis. Multiple trials may be collected and compared to identify trends and evaluate experimental uncertainty.

This simulation is useful for studying static friction, kinetic friction, coefficients of friction, normal force, inclined-plane motion, experimental measurement techniques, data analysis, graph interpretation, and the distinction between the onset of motion and motion at constant speed.
