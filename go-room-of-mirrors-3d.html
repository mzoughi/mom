This simulation explores a room whose walls (and, if you choose, ceiling and floor) are plane mirrors. A light source -- anything from a narrow laser to a wide flashlight beam -- shines into the otherwise dark room. Every reflection is specular: each ray leaves a mirror at the same angle at which it arrived, so the light is never scattered around the room. As a result, the light lands on only a few small spots, and most of what is in the room stays in the dark.

Surfaces that are not mirrors -- the floor, an ordinary wall, the objects -- reflect diffusely. Each lit point sends light in all directions and acts like a weak point source, so a little light reaches the rest of the room and makes it faintly visible. A small lit patch of area `A`, receiving the irradiance `E_0` and reflecting the fraction `rho` of it, produces at a distance `r` the irradiance

`E ~~ (rho E_0 A cos theta_e cos theta_r)/(pi r^2)`,

where `theta_e` and `theta_r` are the angles between the line joining the two surfaces and their normals. The simulation follows this diffuse reflection once, including its images in the mirrors. With diffuse reflection turned off, only the specular path of the beam is followed.

Mirrors do not absorb this scattered light; they pass it on. In a room whose surfaces are all mirrors, the light scattered from the spot where the beam lands keeps reflecting until it reaches the objects, the only surfaces that can absorb it. So, surprisingly, the objects in an all-mirror room glow more brightly than in an ordinary room, where the walls, floor and ceiling absorb most of the scattered light. Only objects that absorb all light, perfectly black ones, would leave nothing but the laser spot to be seen.

The 3-D view shows the room from outside. Mirror surfaces are tinted, and the rays of the beam are traced from the source through every reflection until they land on a surface that is not a mirror, such as the floor or one of the objects. Where they land, the surface is lit. The 3-D view can be turned and zoomed, and it also shows where the viewer stands and which way the viewer looks.

The viewer's screen shows what the viewer's eye actually sees. It is computed by following rays backward from the eye: whenever such a ray meets a mirror, it is reflected and followed further. This is why the lit spots appear many times, once directly and again as images in the mirrors -- the "infinite" corridors of a room of mirrors. With haze in the air, a little light is scattered sideways from the beam itself, so the path of the beam becomes visible.

At every mirror the ray obeys the law of reflection

`theta_r = theta_i`,

which, for a ray travelling in the direction `vec d` toward a mirror whose unit normal is `hat n`, gives the reflected direction

`vec r = vec d - 2 (vec d * hat n) hat n`.

Real mirrors are not perfect. A mirror of reflectivity `R` keeps only the fraction `R` of the light at each reflection, so after `n` reflections the intensity is

`I_n = R^n I_0`.

A flashlight beam also spreads out. A beam of half-angle `alpha` that has travelled a total path `L` (reflections included) has a spot of diameter about

`D ~~ 2 L tan alpha`,

so the same light covers a larger area and each part of the spot is dimmer. A laser has almost no spread, which is why its spot stays small and bright even after many reflections.

The readout follows the central ray of the beam: how many reflections it makes before it lands, its path length, the fraction of its intensity that is left, the diameter of the spot, the angle of incidence at the first mirrors, and the fraction of the room's non-mirror surfaces that the light actually reaches.

Users can change the spread and color of the beam, aim it and move it, choose which surfaces are mirrors and how well they reflect, move the viewer and choose where the viewer looks, and turn on the room lights to see what the dark room really contains. Presets set up a laser, a flashlight, and two facing mirrors that produce a long row of repeated images.

This simulation is useful for studying the law of reflection, specular and diffuse reflection, plane mirrors, images formed by mirrors, multiple reflections, the loss of intensity at each reflection, beam divergence, and why a room of mirrors can be so dark and so confusing at the same time.

  1. Press Laser. The beam reflects many times, yet most of the room stays dark. Explain why, using the readout of the lit part of the room.
  2. Read the angle of incidence at the first mirror. What is the angle between the reflected ray and the mirror's normal? Which law does this show?
  3. With R = 95 %, what fraction of the laser's intensity is left after 10 reflections? Check your answer with the readout.
  4. Press Flashlight. How does the size of the lit spot change as the path length increases? Use D ≈ 2 L tan α to check the spot diameter in the readout.
  5. Press Two mirrors. Describe what the viewer sees and explain where the repeated images come from.
  6. Turn the haze off. Can the viewer still see the beam travelling through the air? Explain why the haze makes it visible.
  7. Choose None (an ordinary room), press Laser, and keep the room lights off. Why can the viewer faintly see the objects, even though the laser lights only one small spot? Turn off the diffuse reflection to compare.
