Telescopes: Refractor, Reflector, Galilean and Monocular

close-up: the image and the eyepiece
what you see: naked eye and through the telescope

A telescope does two things: it gathers far more light than the eye, and it makes distant things look bigger. Light from a star is so far away that its rays arrive parallel. The objective -- a large lens in a refractor, a concave mirror in a reflector -- brings them to a focus, forming a small real image at its focal plane, a distance `f_o` (its focal length) away. The eyepiece, a small lens of focal length `f_e` placed so that this image sits at its own focal point, then sends the light on to the eye as parallel rays again, but at a steeper angle.

Magnification. A star seen at an angle `theta` from the axis forms its image a height `f_o tan theta` from the axis; the eyepiece sends it on at an angle `theta'` with `tan theta' = f_o tan theta / f_e`. For small angles the angular magnification is

`M = theta'/theta = f_o/f_e`.

The image is upside down. A long-focus objective and a short-focus eyepiece give a high magnification. In a refractor the lenses are `f_o + f_e` apart.

Light and detail. The amount of light gathered grows with the area of the aperture `D`: compared with the eye's pupil (about 7 mm in the dark), a telescope collects `(D / 7 "mm")^2` times more. A wider aperture also resolves finer detail: diffraction limits the smallest angle between two stars that can be told apart to about

`theta_R = 1.22 lambda / D`,

about 1.4 seconds of arc for a 100 mm telescope in green light -- though on most nights the turbulence of the atmosphere (seeing) blurs things to about 1 to 2 seconds of arc anyway. Magnifying more than about twice the aperture in millimetres only enlarges the blur. The beam leaving the eyepiece is `D/M` wide (the exit pupil); when that is larger than the eye's pupil some light is wasted, and when it is very small the image becomes dim.

The refractor uses a lens as its objective. A simple lens bends blue light more than red, so the colours come to a focus at different distances: chromatic aberration, which surrounds bright objects with coloured fringes. An achromatic doublet, two lenses of different glasses cemented together, brings two colours to the same focus.

The Galilean telescope has the same converging objective but a diverging (concave) eyepiece of focal length `-|f_e|`, placed before the objective's focus, at `f_o - |f_e|` from the objective. The light never reaches a focus: the eyepiece makes the converging beam parallel again, steeper but on the same side, so the image is upright and `M = -f_o/f_e = +f_o/|f_e|`. Its exit pupil is virtual, inside the tube, so the eye cannot be put at it, and the field of view is narrow. Galileo used this design; so do opera glasses and simple spyglasses.

The monocular (a terrestrial telescope, half of a pair of binoculars) adds an erecting lens. Placed `2 f_r` after the objective's image, it forms a second image `2 f_r` beyond itself, the same size but turned over again -- upright. The eyepiece then magnifies that: `M = (-f_o/f_e)(-1) = +f_o/f_e`, and the length is `f_o + 4 f_r + f_e`. Binoculars do the same job with prisms, which fold the light and keep them short. The numbers on them, such as 8 × 42, are the magnification and the aperture in millimetres; their exit pupil is `42/8 = 5.25` mm.

The reflector (Newton's design) uses a concave mirror at the bottom of the tube. A mirror reflects all colours alike, so it has no chromatic aberration, and large mirrors are much easier to make than large lenses -- which is why all big research telescopes are reflectors. A small flat mirror at 45 degrees near the top sends the light out the side to the eyepiece; it blocks a little of the incoming light (the central obstruction). A spherical mirror does not bring all parallel rays to one point -- spherical aberration -- so telescope mirrors are made parabolic, which focuses parallel rays exactly.
