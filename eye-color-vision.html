This simulation shows how we see color. Light is a mixture of wavelengths, from about 380 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red), but the eye does not measure the whole spectrum. Color vision in daylight relies on just three kinds of cone cell in the retina, named for the wavelengths they respond to best: S (short, near 445 nm), M (medium, near 545 nm) and L (long, near 570 nm).

A light of spectrum `I(lambda)` excites each kind of cone by an amount

`L = int I(lambda) bar l(lambda) d lambda`,   `M = int I(lambda) bar m(lambda) d lambda`,   `S = int I(lambda) bar s(lambda) d lambda`,

where `bar l`, `bar m` and `bar s` are the sensitivity curves of the cones. Everything we know about the color of the light is in these three numbers. The curves overlap a great deal: a light of 580 nm excites the L cones strongly and the M cones somewhat, and the brain reads that ratio as yellow.

Because only three numbers count, very different lights can look exactly the same. A mixture of red and green light excites the cones in the same proportions as yellow light of 580 nm, so it looks yellow, although it contains no yellow light at all. Such lights are called metamers; television and phone screens use them, making every color from just red, green and blue. Mixing light is additive: adding lights adds their cone excitations, and red, green and blue together look white.

Three lights cannot match every color, though. To match some pure wavelengths, one of the three lights would have to be added with a negative amount -- which in a real experiment means adding it to the target instead. This is why the colors of a rainbow are more vivid than any screen can show.

In color vision deficiencies one kind of cone is missing or works abnormally. Without L cones (protanopia) or M cones (deuteranopia), reds and greens that differ only in the excitation of the missing cone look the same; without S cones (tritanopia), blues and yellows are confused. About 8 % of men and 0.5 % of women have a red-green deficiency, mostly in a mild, anomalous form. The simulation shows everything as such a viewer would see it, following Viénot, Brettel and Mollon (1999).

The test plates are made here from the physics: the dots of the hidden number differ from the background only in how strongly they excite one kind of cone. A viewer who lacks that cone cannot tell them apart, so the number disappears.

The colors use the CIE 1931 color matching functions (in the analytic form of Wyman, Sloan and Shirley, 2013), converted to cone responses with the Hunt-Pointer-Estevez transformation. Pure spectral colors lie outside what a screen can show, so they are displayed as closely as the screen allows.
