This simulation shows interference in a thin film: the thin layer of air, or of a liquid of index `n`, between two glass surfaces. Light falling straight down on the film is partly reflected at the top of the gap and partly at its bottom. The second reflection travels an extra distance `2t` in the film, an extra optical path `2nt`. One of the two reflections also has a half-wave (`pi`) phase change: the one where the light meets a medium of higher index. So the reflected light is

dark where `2 n t = m lambda`,   bright where `2 n t = (m + 1/2) lambda`,

and the light that gets through is the opposite: bright where the reflection is dark. The simulation uses the exact result for all the multiple reflections, `R = (F sin^2(delta/2))/(1 + F sin^2(delta/2))`, with `delta = 4 pi n t/lambda` and `F = 4R_1/(1 - R_1)^2`, `R_1` being the reflectance of one glass surface; `R + T = 1`. For an air gap in glass the reflected fringes go from black to bright, but the transmitted ones dip by only about 15 %: they are faint, and the option to stretch the contrast makes them easier to see. When the film has the same index as the glass, the fringes disappear.

The wedge. Two flat plates touch along one edge and are held apart by a thin wire at the other, `D` away. The gap grows steadily, `t = D x/L`, so the fringes are straight and evenly spaced, `Delta x = (lambda L)/(2 n D)`. At the line of contact the reflected light is dark. Counting the fringes measures `D`: `N = (2 n D)/lambda`. Drag the probe to see the two reflected waves at any point, in step or opposed.

Newton's rings. A slightly curved lens of radius `R` rests on a flat plate. The gap grows as `t = r^2/(2R)`, so the fringes are rings, crowding closer outward. The dark rings, seen in reflection, have radii `r_m = sqrt((m lambda R)/n)`. With white light every wavelength makes its own rings, and they overlap in colour, as in a soap film.
