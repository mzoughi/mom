This simulation follows, ray by ray, how a pinhole camera forms an image. An object -- a clown, or three colored lamps -- is lit, so every point of it sends light in all directions. Three points are followed: the clown's green ear, red suit and yellow shoe (or the three lamps). Each sends a fan of rays toward a barrier with an opening and a screen behind it.

Light travels in straight lines, and every ray stops at the first thing it meets. Rays that meet the barrier are stopped; only the rays that pass through the opening go on to the screen. The strip on the right shows what the screen looks like: the colored spots where the rays land, with overlapping colors added together.

Without the barrier, or with the screen in front of it, every point of the screen receives light from every point of the object. The colors mix everywhere and there is no image. The barrier is what makes the image possible: with a small opening, the light reaching each point of the screen comes from only one small part of the object.

The rays through the opening cross there, so the light from a high point lands low on the screen and the light from a low point lands high: the image is upside down. By similar triangles, an object at a distance `d_o` in front of the opening has an image at a distance `d_i` behind it with the magnification

`m = -d_i/d_o`,

so an object of height `h_o` has an image of height `h_i = |m| h_o`.

An opening of size `a` lets through a whole bundle of rays from each point, which lands as a spot rather than a point. Again by similar triangles, the spot has the size

`b = a (1 + d_i/d_o)`.

When the opening is small the spots are small and separate, and the image is sharp but dim. When the opening is wide the spots grow and overlap, so the image is brighter but blurry. The strip can also show the whole picture of the object, built from every one of its points, to see this sharpness and blur directly.

Drag the object, the barrier, the two edges of the opening, or the screen; or use the sliders. The presets show a pinhole, a wide opening, no barrier, and the screen placed in front of the barrier. The yellow box shows the coordinates of the pointer.

This simulation is useful for understanding the straight-line propagation of light, why a pinhole camera needs a barrier, why its image is inverted, how its size depends on the distances, and why a larger opening gives a brighter but blurrier image.
