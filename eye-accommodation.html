This simulation shows how the eye focuses on objects at different distances. A camera focuses by moving its lens; the eye cannot, so instead it changes the shape of its lens. This is accommodation.

The lens hangs inside a ring of muscle, the ciliary muscle, by thin fibres called zonules. When the muscle is relaxed, the ring is wide and the zonules pull the lens flat: the eye is focused far away. When the muscle contracts, the ring narrows, the zonules slacken, and the elastic lens bulges into a rounder shape with more focusing power.

The simulation uses the reduced eye, with a power of 60 D. An object at a distance `d` sends light that diverges with a vergence of `-1/d`; to bring it to a focus on the retina, the eye must add

`A_"needed" = R + 1/d`

diopters of accommodation, where `R` is the refractive error of the eye (zero for a normal eye, negative for a nearsighted eye, positive for a farsighted one). The most the eye can add is its amplitude of accommodation, `A_"max"`. The range of clear vision runs from the near point to the far point:

`d_"near" = 1/(A_"max" - R)`,   `d_"far" = -1/R`  (or infinity if `R >= 0`).

The amplitude falls steadily with age, as the lens hardens. On average (Hofstetter),

`A_"max" ~~ 18.5 - 0.30 * "age"`,

from about 15 D at age 10 (a near point of 7 cm) to about 5 D at 45 (20 cm) and almost nothing after 60. When the near point moves beyond comfortable reading distance, about 40 cm, reading glasses are needed: this is presbyopia, and it happens to everyone.

A nearsighted eye has a far point at a finite distance, `-1/R`; beyond it, nothing is sharp however relaxed the lens. A farsighted eye must accommodate even for distant objects, using up part of its amplitude, so its near point is farther away.

An object outside the range of clear vision focuses in front of or behind the retina and makes a blur circle. Its angular size, in minutes of arc, is shown with what the eye sees; normal sharp vision resolves about 1 minute of arc.
