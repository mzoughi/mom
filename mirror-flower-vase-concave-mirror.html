Mirror equation 1/do + 1/di = 1/f  |  f = R/2  |  m = −di/do  |  At do = R: di = R and m = −1, so the hidden upside-down flower is imaged upright, same size, where the vase stands

This simulation demonstrates the classic "flower in the vase" optical illusion produced by a concave mirror. A hidden upside-down flower is placed inside a dark enclosure below a vase, while a concave mirror forms a real image of the flower that appears to float inside the otherwise empty vase. The simulation illustrates image formation by concave mirrors and explains why the observer perceives an upright flower even though the actual flower is hidden from view.

The main side-view diagram shows the hidden flower, the concave mirror, the vase, and the observer. Principal rays originating from the flower are traced to the mirror and reflected according to the law of reflection. The reflected rays converge to form a real image at a location above the hidden flower and inside the vase.

A separate viewer's view panel shows what an observer would actually see. When the optical system is properly aligned, the flower appears to be suspended within the vase even though no physical flower is present there. This creates the illusion that the vase contains a real flower.

Users can adjust the focal length of the concave mirror and the position of the flower stand relative to the mirror. As these parameters change, the image location, magnification, and appearance of the illusion update automatically. The simulation continuously applies the mirror equation to calculate the image distance and image properties.

A movable observer allows users to investigate how the illusion appears from different viewing positions. The distance of the eye from the mirror and the height of the eye above the shelf can be adjusted to explore lines of sight and determine whether the image remains visible.

The simulation displays important optical quantities including the focal length `f`, object distance `d_o`, image distance `d_i`, magnification `m`, and image type. These values update in real time as the geometry of the system changes.

Several viewing options allow users to display or hide principal rays, the image of the vase, the focal point F, the center of curvature C, and the illumination source used to light the hidden flower. These controls help visualize how the image is formed and how the illusion works.

Buttons provide quick access to important configurations, such as placing the stand at the center of curvature C or at the focal point F. These settings allow users to investigate special mirror positions and compare the resulting image characteristics.

The simulation emphasizes the mirror equation

`1/d_o + 1/d_i = 1/f`

and the magnification relationship

`m = -d_i/d_o`.

These equations are used to determine the location, orientation, and size of the image formed by the concave mirror.

Users can explore how the image changes between real and virtual images, how magnification varies with object position, and why a real image can appear to occupy empty space. The simulation provides both a geometric ray-tracing representation and a realistic view of what an observer would see.

This simulation is useful for studying concave mirrors, real and virtual images, magnification, focal length, center of curvature, principal rays, geometric optics, mirror equations, image formation, and optical illusions based on real-image projection.

  1. Press Stand at C. Where does the image of the flower form, and is it real or virtual? How does its size compare with the flower?
  2. Why must the flower be hung upside down for it to appear upright in the vase?
  3. Turn the lamp off. What does the viewer see now, and why?
  4. The mirror also forms an image of the vase. Where does it form, and why does the viewer not see it?
  5. Move the stand slowly from C toward F. Describe what happens to the position and the size of the image.
  6. Put the stand between F and the mirror. Describe the image now. Can it appear in the vase?
  7. With the stand at C, raise and lower the viewer's eye. At what heights does the flower disappear? Use the rays reaching the eye to explain why.
