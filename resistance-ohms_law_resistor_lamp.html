Choose the carbon resistor or the incandescent light bulb. For the carbon resistor, set its resistance (at 20 °C) and its starting (ambient) temperature; it heats up a little as current flows, and its resistance falls slightly with temperature. For the light bulb, set its rated wattage, which determines its cold resistance (a higher wattage means a lower resistance), and its starting temperature.

The circuit: a low-voltage power supply (LVPS, the box at the bottom) drives a current through the device at the top. An ammeter (A, on the right) in series measures the current I; a voltmeter (V) connected across the device measures the voltage V across it. Their readings are written under the meters, and listed under the drawings. Set the supply voltage or the current (one follows the other), then press Collect data point: the readings, with a small random measurement error, are added to the graph of V against I.

Ohmic or not. For an ohmic device the resistance R = V/I does not depend on the current: the graph is a straight line through the origin, and its slope is R. “Show the linear fit” gives the best straight line V = mI + b, with the uncertainties in the slope and the intercept (from the scatter of the points) and R². Devices heat up as current flows: a metal’s resistance rises with temperature, a semiconductor’s falls. A lamp filament heats up a lot, so its graph curves up; a diode hardly conducts until about 0.6 V (2 to 3 V for an LED) and then the current rises steeply. “Show the theoretical curve” draws the model’s curve, and “Explore the slope at” draws its tangent: its slope is the dynamic resistance dV/dI at that point.

The Average tab gives the mean, the standard deviation and the standard error of V/I, V, I or the power P = IV over the points collected. The data stay saved in this browser, separately for each device, also when answers are submitted. The resistance is never shown directly: find it from your data.
