Close your left eye and look straight at the + with your right eye. Start with your face about 50 cm from the screen and slowly move closer: at some distance the dot disappears. Its image has landed on your optic disc, where there are no rods or cones. (The test works best with the dot and the cross about 8 cm apart on your screen.)

This simulation compares the eye with a camera. Light from a clown nearby and from a tree farther away enters the eye; the side view traces rays from the top of each, and the second panel shows the image they form on the retina, at its real size.

Light travels in straight lines, so the rays from the top of an object reach the bottom of the retina, and those from the left reach the right: the image on the retina is upside down and reversed. The brain turns it the right way around. (Tick the box to see it as the brain presents it.)

A pinhole. Without a lens, a small hole alone forms an image, as in a camera obscura. Every point of the object makes a small patch of light the size of the hole, so a bigger hole gives a brighter but blurrier image. For an object at distance `d_o` and a retina at distance `d_i` behind a hole of diameter `a`, the patch has a diameter

`b = a (1 + d_i/d_o)`.

A very small hole does not help either: light spreading out by diffraction makes a spot of diameter about `2.44 lambda d_i/a`. The best hole balances the two, `a ~~ sqrt(2.44 lambda d_i)`, about 0.2 mm for an eye -- far too dim for most purposes.

A lens. The cornea and the lens bend the light so that all the rays from one point of the object meet again at one point. The pupil can then be wide, letting in much more light, and the image is still sharp, as long as it falls on the retina. The simulation uses the reduced eye: a single curved surface with a power of about 60 D, between air and a medium of index `n' = 1.336`, with the retina 22.2 mm behind it. An object at a distance `d` focuses at

`(n')/v = P + 1/u`,   with `u = -d`,

and the eye accommodates by adding up to about 10 D to `P` for near objects. An object at another distance focuses in front of or behind the retina, and makes a blur circle of diameter

`b = D_p |v - v_R|/v`,

proportional to the pupil diameter `D_p`. This is depth of field: with a small pupil a wide range of distances looks sharp, at the price of a dimmer image. The f-number of the eye, `N = f/D_p`, is about 4 for a 4 mm pupil, as in a camera.

The image on the retina is small: an object that spans an angle `theta` makes an image about `17 " mm" * tan theta` long. 1 mm on the retina corresponds to about 3.4 degrees.

The blind spot. About 15 degrees from the fovea, toward the nose, the optic nerve leaves the eye. There are no rods or cones there, so an image falling on that spot is not seen. The test card lets you find your own blind spot.
