Law of reflection θr = θi  |  image distance = object distance, image height = object height (m = 1)  |  to see yourself from head to toe, the mirror must be half your height, whatever your distance

This simulation shows, in a side view, a plane mirror on a wall. The object (a clown or an arrow) and an eye are in front of the mirror, on the left; the region behind the mirror, on the right, is where the image appears. No light ever goes there: the image is virtual.

At the mirror every ray obeys the law of reflection: the reflected ray makes the same angle with the normal (the line perpendicular to the mirror) as the incident ray,

`theta_r = theta_i`.

The rays that leave one point of the object and reflect from the mirror spread out again, as if they came from a single point behind the mirror. Tracing them back finds that point: the image. For a plane mirror the image is as far behind the mirror as the object is in front of it, and it has the same size:

`d_i = d_o`,   `h_i = h_o`.

An image can only be seen if light from the object actually reaches the eye. For each point of the object, the light that reaches the eye leaves the mirror along the line from the image point to the eye. That line must cross the mirror itself; where it would cross the wall beside the mirror, the light simply is not there. The lines from the eye through the top and bottom of the mirror enclose the part of the region behind the mirror that the eye can see; only the part of the image inside it is visible.

When you look at yourself, the light from the top of your head and the light from your feet must both reach your eye. By similar triangles, the light from your head reflects halfway between your eye and the top of your head, and the light from your feet halfway between your eye and your feet. So the smallest mirror in which you can see all of yourself is

`h_"mirror" = h/2`,

half your height, with its top halfway between your eyes and the top of your head. Remarkably, this does not depend on how far from the mirror you stand.

The rays can show where the image is (two rays from the top and two from the bottom of the object, with the normals and the rays traced back), a fan of rays from a light point that can be dragged anywhere (leave a trace to write with the light, and watch the mirror image of your writing appear), or only the light that reaches the eye. The panel on the right of the readout shows what the eye actually sees in the mirror.

This simulation is useful for studying the law of reflection, how a plane mirror forms a virtual image, why light has to reach the eye for an image to be seen, and how tall a mirror needs to be.

  1. Press Where is the image? Move the object closer to the mirror and farther away. How does the distance of the image behind the mirror compare with the distance of the object in front of it? How do their heights compare?
  2. Press A fan of rays and drag the light point. Where do the reflected rays seem to come from? What happens to the rays that miss the mirror?
  3. Press Can the eye see it? Make the mirror shorter, or move the eye. Which parts of the clown can the eye still see, and why?
  4. Press How tall a mirror? How tall must the mirror be for the clown to see itself from head to toe? Compare with the clown's height.
  5. Move the clown closer to the mirror and farther away. Does the mirror it needs change? Explain with `h_"mirror" = h/2`.
  6. Turn on the trace and write your name with the light point. What does its image look like?
