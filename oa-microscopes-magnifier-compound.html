Microscopes: the Magnifier, the Compound Microscope and its Objectives

a lab microscope, with its optics drawn beside it · click the nosepiece to change the objective

This simulation shows how magnifiers and microscopes make small things look bigger, and what limits the detail they can show.

How big something looks depends on the angle it takes up at the eye. Without help, the closest we can see an object clearly is the near point, about 25 cm away; so the most an object of height `h` can take up is `theta = h/(25 "cm")`. An instrument's angular magnification compares the angle its image takes up, `theta'`, with that: `M = theta'/theta`.

1. The magnifier. A converging lens with the object inside its focal length `f` forms an upright, virtual, enlarged image. With the eye close to the lens, the image takes up the same angle as the object seen from the lens, so `M = (25 "cm")/d_o`. With the object at the focal point the image is at infinity, seen with a relaxed eye: `M = (25 "cm")/f`. Bringing the object a little closer puts the image at the near point: `M = 1 + (25 "cm")/f`. A single lens is hard to use beyond about 5 times.

2. The compound microscope uses two converging lenses. The objective, of short focal length `f_o`, has the specimen just outside its focal point and forms a real, inverted, enlarged first image:

`1/d_i = 1/f_o - 1/d_o`,   `m_o = -d_i/d_o`.

The eyepiece, a magnifier of focal length `f_e`, a distance `L` from the objective, looks at that image, which is `d_o' = L - d_i` in front of it. It forms the final, virtual image at `1/d_i' = 1/f_e - 1/d_o'`, with `m_e = -d_i'/d_o'`. The overall linear magnification is the product, `m = m_o m_e`. How much bigger the specimen looks, with the eye at the eyepiece, compared with the specimen at 25 cm, is `M = |m_o| (25 "cm")/d_o'`. A microscope is focused by moving the whole tube (here, the specimen), keeping `L` fixed. Focused for a relaxed eye, the first image sits at the eyepiece's focal point, and

`M = -(L - f_o - f_e)/f_o * (25 "cm")/f_e`,

where `L - f_o - f_e`, the distance between the objective's image-side focal point and the eyepiece's object-side focal point, is the tube length, standardized at 16 cm. The image is upside down.

3. Objectives and resolution. No matter how much a microscope magnifies, it cannot show detail finer than diffraction allows. The light-gathering power of an objective is its numerical aperture, `NA = n sin alpha`, where `alpha` is half the angle of the cone of light it accepts from the specimen and `n` the refractive index between them. Two points closer than about

`d = 0.61 lambda / NA`

blur into one (the Rayleigh criterion). In air `NA` cannot exceed 1; and rays leaving the cover glass at steep angles are trapped by total internal reflection. Filling the gap with water or immersion oil (`n = 1.51`, close to that of glass) lets those rays into the objective: `NA` up to about 1.4, and resolution near 0.2 micrometres. Magnifying more than about 500 to 1000 times the `NA` only enlarges the blur.
